Part 2: The flight instrument

Part 2

The core of the app, and the part people already know from other instruments.

The point to land in this part: all of this is computed on the phone, so it works even when the internet is gone.

The instrument

One screenshot, six numbers. Walk through them up top, from the left: - ALTITUDE and AGL: height above sea level and above the ground. - FLIGHT TIME and SPEED. - GLIDE 10S: glide ratio over the last ten seconds. - XC SCORE and WIND.

At the bottom: the map with your track, the other pilots (Vegar, Svein, Tom S), and the contour lines. The profile at the very bottom is the terrain under you.

Say the point: none of these numbers come from the internet. They come from the GPS and the barometer in the phone.

Thermals and vario

The single most convincing clip, and three things happen at once: the vario beeps, the app draws the thermal bubble, and the wind arrow tightens while you circle.

Say it as one point: it doesn’t know where the lift is when you take off. It learns it from your circles, and it remembers where the lift was.

This is a recording of the app playing back a real flight. Same code in replay as live, so what you see is exactly what you get in the air.

Plan a task

Do this live if you can. The slide is your backup.

The task on the image is real Voss terrain. Point at the names: - TAKEOFF Bavallen - START Storebrekkuna, 986 m, the cylinder is the dashed circle - GOAL Bjørnestigen - LANDING Rokne - TASK DIST 95.9 km, and L/D 8.0 on the route

The cylinders and the dashed legs are drawn straight onto the map. The side view at the bottom shows the terrain profile under the route, so you see what you have to get over.

Send it to the group, and ask them to look at their phone. The notification is called “Load task”. Wait for the sound in the room before you move on.

After the flight

One slide that does four jobs, so you don’t need one per thing: - Look at the flight: the map, the track, where you flew. - The text and photos you added yourself. - The segments: Ground and Flying separated automatically. - The numbers: distance, vertical meters, time.

Talk over it about what happens next: IGC out, automatic upload to Flightlog and XContest, and dedup against what’s already there.

This is the reason people open the app again on Tuesday.

Activity detection

  • Detects flying, hiking, ground
  • Mode under Menu, My status
    • Automatic: hike and fly
    • Auto takeoff and landing: flights only
  • Wrong? Flight summary, Activity, Edit
    • “It was all one flight”, or split at the playhead
    • Tap a row to relabel, Undo to step back
  • Answers “40 min flight saved as three short trips”
  • Segments are the flight: airtime, distance, XC score come from them
  • Every edit recalculates the numbers, and trains the detector

AreaContest

The territories game. Fly a loop around a piece of terrain, and you own what’s inside. Fly straight, and you take a belt on each side of the track, and the belt gets wider the further you dare go from launch.

Every time you cover the same ground again, your claim gets one notch stronger. Whoever owns a valley is whoever keeps flying it.

The best retention mechanic in the app, and it costs you nothing to mention. You can open pgpilot.app/areacontest live if the internet holds.

Part 3: Live wind

Part 3

The wind is the hardest measurement in the whole app, because we don’t have a wind meter. This part is about where the numbers come from, and how far you can trust them.

Map over Voss

The map over Voss, as the audience sees it on the phone. Takeoffs, the contours, and the other pilots live on the map.

This is the screen you start from. Tap a takeoff marker and the wind comes up.

Tap Live wind

What comes up when you tap the takeoff marker: the wind meter standing right next to the takeoff, Hangur East here.

Each row is a 15-minute average, with gusts in parentheses and the arrow showing the direction. The blue NOW bar separates the measurements from the forecast, and the met.no row below sits dimmed next to it, so you see at once whether the model nailed the day or missed it.

The airgram

The airgram: the forecast for takeoff. The yellow columns are the thermals, darker is stronger, and the top of the column is as high as you’ll get. Wind arrows up through the heights, clouds and sun in the rows at the top, rain as blue streaks.

Part 4: Comms

Part 4

The new stuff, and the part that makes people stay in the app.

Say it explicitly: everyone listens, one talks.

Talk together

Push-to-talk over the internet. You say something, and it comes out of every phone in the group at the same time, with no range limit.

This is the moment the room understands what the app is. Ask a volunteer to press and talk, and everyone hears it.

The channel is called Local in the group. Everyone listens, one talks.

The button on the riser

  • Hold to talk
  • Double-click to zoom in
  • Triple-click to zoom out
  • Tap and hold for the agent

The little black button in the list, the PTT-Z01, sits on the riser, within thumb’s reach. Hands stay on the brakes, gloves stay on, and the phone stays where it is.

Four presses, show them with your hand while you talk: - Hold: talk. - Double-click: zoom in. - Triple-click: zoom out. - Tap, then hold: the voice assistant. “Navigate to Jørgen”, “what’s the wind”.

Zoom is the one people underestimate. In the air you don’t switch numbers, you switch the view: in when you’re working a bubble, out when you’re deciding where to go next.

Two things that surprise: - The screen can be dimmed. Hold the button a little longer than half a second and the screen wakes AND the transmission starts. - The button works even when pgpilot isn’t in front.

Everything can be remapped, and you can pair more than one button. This is Bluetooth, so it doesn’t exist in the browser version.

The KV4P radio

  • Transmits on VHF and the pgpilot channel at the same time
  • Repeater: turn on "Relay the group out over the radio"
  • Dedup: tries to play each transmission once
  • Bluetooth, v2.0

A radio that connects to the phone, instead of a 90s gadget with incomprehensible menus.

This is how it works in pgpilot: you connect the radio and transmit on both VHF and the pgpilot channel. You hear both your modern “internet” friends and your remoter or old-fashioned friends on VHF.

The device can even act as a repeater: it can relay a VHF message into the pgpilot group, and the group out over VHF. That direction is off by default - flip Relay the group out over the radio in the radio settings to turn it on.

We also have an early dedup: if you hear the same transmission over both radio and internet, the app tries to play it only once.

The benefits of having the radio on the phone: - No fumbling with bad radio menus. - We can use radio only when there’s no coverage. - Headphones pair like with any phone. - We can run far better noise cancelling on a phone than the old radios do.

kv4p.com has the list of certified radios. Make sure you get the 2.0 version. (The 868 MHz advice in part 6 is for the LoRa boards, not this radio.)

Part 6: The Bir pack aka off grid

Part 6

The last part, and it’s about what carries you when the internet doesn’t.

Bir is a valley in India where the local community flies with its own radios. That’s why this pack exists, and that’s why it’s interesting back home too: when the internet disappears, this is what’s left. No forecast, no airspace, and nobody sees where you are.

Bir itself adds the rest: many landings have no road out, hardly any flat ground, and no rescue on call. That’s why the valley runs its own radios.

Three radios, three jobs

VHF

Voice, when nothing else works. The KV4P radio from part 4.

LoRa

Position and short text with no infrastructure. FANET and Meshtastic.

Satellite

When there's no coverage at all. InReach.

The frame for the whole part. Three technologies, three different jobs, and the app talks to all three.

VHF is for voice. LoRa is for position and short messages. Satellite is the last resort.

VHF you already saw in part 4, so the next slides take the other two.

FANET and FLARM

  • Position every second
  • Landed and distress status
  • Short text
  • Local weather stations

FANET with FLARM is what varios like Skytraxx and SoftRF trackers broadcast. It’s what the OGN receivers on the ridges listen for.

With the radio paired under Settings, Connected devices, you get: - Every pilot the radio hears, on the map and in Nearby, with names where we know them. If the phone has internet, it relays them to the pgpilot backend, so friends without a radio see them too, and your own position reaches the FANET/OGN world. - A pilot who landed, needs a ride, or pressed the distress button shows up as exactly that, not as an aircraft with a heading.

Turn the layer on in the map settings. It’s the “Show live OGN traffic on map” switch in the picture.

Meshtastic and Bircom

  • Its own mesh, with no ground stations
  • Channels as their own rows in Comms
  • Works with no cell signal

Meshtastic is the general-purpose mesh. Messages hop from radio to radio, so a valley full of pilots is its own network.

In Bir the local community runs Bircom on top of it, and most pilots there fly with a Meshtastic radio in the harness.

With the radio paired you get: - Meshtastic channels as their own rows in Comms. That chat works with no cell signal at all, it only needs the Bluetooth link to the radio. - FANET text the radio hears is relayed into the Local chat, so a pilot with FANET only reaches everyone in the valley, not just the phone that heard them. - Meshtastic nodes from Bir show up on the map. The switch is called “Show Bircom mesh nodes”.

You don’t need the Meshtastic app on the phone. If you’ve used it, close it first: the radio only takes one Bluetooth client at a time.

InReach

Garmin InReach MapShare

Under Connected services you enter your MapShare name, the part after share.garmin.com/, once.

Inbound, not outbound

The app polls the MapShare feed and pulls your satellite position into pgpilot as its own source. It is not a channel we transmit to, and not Bluetooth messages or SOS.

For the others

Your position lands on everyone else's map even where there's no cell coverage at all, and someone at home can follow the dot.

InReach is the last resort, and it’s important to explain it right:

This is inbound. The app polls the Garmin MapShare KML feed and pulls your position from the satellite into pgpilot, as its own source. It is not a channel we transmit to, and it’s not Bluetooth messages or SOS.

You enter your MapShare name, the part after share.garmin.com/, once. After that the others see you on the map even where there’s no cell coverage at all, and someone at home can follow the dot.

In short: VHF for voice, LoRa for position and text, InReach when nothing else gets through.

Which LoRa board

SenseCAP T1000-E

Credit-card size, waterproof, GPS and battery built in. The one I'd pick, simply because it disappears in a harness pocket.

LILYGO T-Echo

Bigger, with an e-paper screen, a larger battery and an external antenna. Which is why the Bir community made it their standard. Pick this if range and battery matter more than size.

Anything with an nRF52840 and an SX1262 or LR1110 works. Avoid boards with the older SX127x radio, they have poor range.

  • SenseCAP T1000-E. Credit-card size, waterproof, GPS and battery built in. Recommended for size.
  • LILYGO T-Echo. Bigger, e-paper, bigger battery, external antenna. The Bir standard.

Both ship with Meshtastic. If you want FANET instead, you flash SoftRF onto the same board, and pgpilot sees it as a FANET/FLARM tracker.

Pick 868 MHz explicitly if you fly in Europe or India. The listings default to 915 or 433.
